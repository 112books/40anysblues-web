The portraits in **40 anys de blues a Barcelona** were made in the studio, with an analogue camera, black-and-white film and hand processing by the photographer himself. The first sessions took place in the author's studio in Poble Sec ([Llumàtics](https://llumatics.com)); after the pandemic the studio moved into the Nau Bostik, in the La Sagrera neighbourhood. Each musician stood in front of the lens for one day, in a portrait session agreed without haste, and what has survived is the digitisation of the negatives: one medium-format square, which goes on the left-hand page, and one 8×10, which goes on the right-hand page. Digitisation and editing are also the author's own responsibility, with minimal intervention to remove dust marks and adjust contrast.

## What large format photography is

Large format photography uses negatives far bigger than those of medium or small format: 4×5, 5×7 or 8×10 inches, that is, between 100 and 500 times the area of a 35 mm frame. The bigger the negative, the more information it carries: more detail in the midtones, more shades of grey and a texture unlike anything a digital sensor produces.

In exchange, large format is slow. The camera sits on a tripod, the viewfinder is covered with a dark cloth, each sheet is loaded by hand and the photographer has to settle the exposure before releasing the shutter. That slowness is precisely what matters in a portrait: it forces you to look at the musician, to talk to them and to wait for the gesture.

## Medium format and large format in the archive

The project combines two formats. Most of the portraits were made in **6×6 medium format** with a Hasselblad 500 C/M, a legendary 1957 camera that gives a 6 × 6 centimetre square and forces you to compose inside a closed frame. It is the most agile format for a studio portrait session and the one that gives the archive its visual unity.

The **8×10 large format** portrait was made with a Horseman, using the largest negative that can be handled comfortably inside a studio. It is the slowest moment of the session and the one that allows the portrait to be enlarged to wall size without losing anything.

The procedure was always the same. First, one roll of film or more with the Hasselblad: the agile format, the one that lets you warm up, see how each musician reacts and, above all, establish a bond of trust. Once the session had settled down, the Horseman was set up and the large-format portrait was made. So for each musician twelve 6 × 6 frames and two 8×10 plates were used.

Later, when it comes to choosing, the difference shows. Often the two versions of the same musician do not look alike: as a rule, the large-format picture is the more serious one, while the medium-format one could be more relaxed and unorthodox. The author did not want the same kind of immortalisation in two different formats.

## The process: film, development and printing

Every session used fine-grain black-and-white film —Rollei RPX 100 and Foma 100 and 400, with Ilford HP5 only occasionally— developed in the author's own darkroom with classic Rodinal, working up the density each film needs for a portrait. The result is a raw negative, with visible grain and contained highlights, the opposite of retouching: nothing is hidden here.

Chemical photography is therefore a way of working and also a way of looking. The blues was born as a poor music, made with simple instruments and whatever was at hand; the second-hand camera, the out-of-date film and the home darkroom are the same gesture applied to the image.

## The sessions and the people in them

The 148 profiles in the archive carry the date of the portrait session, made in the Barcelona studio between 2020 and 2026. Each profile holds the portrait, the biography written by [Manuel López Poy](/en/autors/manuel-lopez-poy/) and the bands the musician has played with.
